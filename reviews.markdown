### 15-213 Introduction to Computer Systems
{: #course-15-213 }
Nickname: two-thirteen, systems\\
Professor: David Andersen, Nathan Beckmann, Brian Railing\\
Units: 12\\
In-class: 4 hours/week\\
Homework: avg 10 hours/week
- Programming Assignment = ~6-12 hours/week
- Written Assignment = 1 hour/week

**Total work: 12-16 hours/week**\\
**Difficulty: 8/10**\\
**Rating: 8/10**\\
I've always wanted to know how computer worked, I think I've been quite curious about the hardware aspect (which we didn't really get into) but just inner workings of computer systems, like virtual memory, caches, memory allocation, and especially proxies (networking and system input/output) was really interesting to me. I especially loved how we got to learn and apply the concepts hands-on in programming labs where we often simulated or built our own simple version of these concepts, like a simple proxy or file system or cache simulator or dynamic memory allocator and it really got me used to how it actually works. Very cool! Also very time consuming though... debugging was quite stressful, and I don't have a lot of experience writing large chunks of code in a systematic manner. So during cachelab, I went in without much of a plan, and when my trashy implementation of the simulator didn't work and I couldn't figure out why it lowkey had me in tears, especially since I was on a self-imposed tight schedule and the lab just threw my schedule off the rails. I took a step back and didn't touch it for a couple days, then took a late day (my first ever late day at CMU) and restarted with a blank page and a plan drawn out in my notebook. Definitely learned a lot about modularity and good programming practices from that, hah hah. Went to office hours often for the labs which definitely helped with my programming and conceptual understanding. I feel like the lectures went too fast and crammed a ton of information at once, so most of my understanding came from hands-on application from the labs and reviewing the slides myself. But overall, great class, I've heard that this class was one of the best systems CS courses across colleges by teaching advanced stuff in freshman year and a lot of other universities use this cirriculum as well, and I'm not surprised. But it's not difficult to get an A if you put in the time and work for this one. The grading is pretty chill, and most of it comes just from doing the labs. Midterm and final were not bad. 


### 15-150 Principles of Functional Programming
{: #course-15-150 }
Nickname: Functional, 150\\
Professors: Michael Erdmann and Dilsun Kaynar\\
Units: 12\\
In-class: 4.5 hours/week\\
Homework: avg 6-9 hours/week

**Total work: 10-13 hours/week**\\
**Difficulty: 8.5/10**\\
**Rating: 6.5/10**\\
At first I found this class quite boring, it just seemed like learning the syntax and semantics of SML with a ton of rules like typing, totality, valuability, extensional equivalence, etc which I didn't find very enjoyable. Later on, with higher order functinos, continuation passing style, imperative, streams, and sequences, I found it much more enjoyable. I quite liked some of the coding because it was in the form of small puzzles, with often very clean solutions, and the programming really helped me understand the concepts. The lectures I found pretty boring sometimes, and I slept through them quite often... but I think it definitely made me a better programmer and taught me to twist my mind in interesting ways. Overall, I enjoyed the puzzles, the content was bland at times but I see how this class is useful. Quite a bit of work though, the programming/written took a good chunk of time and I had to study for two midterms.

Units: 12\\

Honestly a great class, at first I hated the checkins because they were soooo stressful but I think I'm pretty good at approaching stressful tests with a good mentality of "okay, let's see what I can do.
